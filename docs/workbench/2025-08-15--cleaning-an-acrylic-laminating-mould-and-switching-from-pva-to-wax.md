# Cleaning an acrylic laminating mould and switching from PVA to wax

## Context

An acrylic sheet had been used as the mould surface for carbon-fibre lamination with PVA release agent. The original question was how to remove PVA, cured epoxy fragments, and general residue while preserving a smooth, reusable mould surface.

During later capture review, the actual process history was updated:

- acetone had been used heavily to clean the mould; and
- the release system had been changed from PVA solution to wax.

No resulting crazing, clouding, softening, distortion, surface damage, demoulding performance, wax product, or wax application schedule was reported.

## Advice received for the original PVA process

Because PVA release film is water-soluble, the response recommended:

1. wet the mould with warm water;
2. leave it for approximately 5–10 minutes so the PVA softens;
3. wipe gently with a soft sponge or microfibre cloth;
4. re-wet stubborn areas rather than applying aggressive force;
5. lift loose cured-epoxy flakes with a plastic card or plastic scraper if needed;
6. rinse with clean water; and
7. dry with a clean, non-abrasive cloth.

The response concluded that a solvent should not be necessary for removing PVA.

## Unadopted peel-film proposal

For future PVA use, the response suggested applying several heavier coats to create a continuous film that might peel away after demoulding. The intended sequence was to lift an edge with a fingernail or plastic card, peel the film with attached residue, and finish with a damp wipe.

This method was not tried or adopted. The process instead moved away from PVA and towards wax.

## Actual later process

### Acetone cleaning

Heavy acetone use is recorded as an action taken, not as a recommended acrylic-cleaning method.

Acetone is generally aggressive towards PMMA acrylic and can cause surface softening, clouding, dissolution, or stress crazing. Whether damage occurred in this mould was not reported. The exact sheet material and current surface condition therefore remain important unknowns.

### Switch to wax release

Wax became the current release approach. This changes the recurring mould-maintenance problem from washing away a water-soluble PVA film to managing a thin wax layer:

- apply the selected product according to its own instructions;
- allow the required haze or cure time;
- buff without scratching or contaminating the mould;
- establish the number of coats needed for a prepared or newly restored surface;
- renew the film on a defined schedule; and
- use only cleaning products compatible with both the mould material and subsequent wax application.

These are process requirements rather than a recorded wax procedure; the product and working method were not supplied.

## Editorial notes

- Follow the PVA or wax manufacturer's coating thickness, coat count, drying, buffing, and reapplication instructions rather than generic quantities.
- “Two or three heavy wet coats” of PVA could run, remain wet, transfer texture, or impair release.
- PVA can be washed away with water but is not guaranteed to peel as one intact skin from every acrylic mould.
- High-concentration isopropyl alcohol can also contribute to crazing in some acrylic grades and stressed parts and should not be treated as universally benign.
- Avoid heat-gun assistance on an acrylic mould; local heating can distort the surface or introduce stress.
- Metal scrapers can scratch the working face and were not recommended.
- Toothpaste is an uncontrolled abrasive rather than a predictable acrylic-restoration compound.
- Peel ply is designed to leave a textured composite surface and is not a smooth disposable mould barrier.
- Cling film can wrinkle, while packing-tape seams and adhesive can transfer into the part or contaminate the mould.
- Any solvent or polishing compound used before waxing must be fully removed and shown not to attack the sheet.

## Open checks

- Confirm whether the sheet is PMMA acrylic or another transparent plastic.
- Inspect under strong directional light for haze, fine crazing, local softening, scratches, and loss of flatness after acetone exposure.
- Record the selected release-wax product and its substrate compatibility.
- Record initial coat count, application and buffing method, cure time, and renewal interval.
- Track whether demoulding force or surface quality changes across repeated laminations.
